# Capital Expenditures (CapEx) Explained

## 1. What Capital Expenditures Represent

**Capital Expenditures (CapEx)** on the **cash flow statement**
represent the **cash a company spends to acquire or improve long‑term
assets** such as equipment, buildings, machinery, or vehicles.

These assets typically provide economic benefit for **more than one
year**.

Typical examples include:

-   Buying manufacturing equipment\
-   Building a factory\
-   Purchasing company vehicles\
-   Major upgrades to machinery or facilities

CapEx **does not include routine operating expenses**, such as
maintenance, salaries, or utilities.

On the cash flow statement it usually appears in the **Investing
Activities** section as:

-   *Purchase of Property and Equipment*
-   *Purchases of PP&E*
-   *Capital Expenditures*

It is usually shown as a **negative number (cash outflow)**.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 2. Is CapEx a GAAP Number?

Not exactly.

GAAP requires companies to report **cash flows from investing
activities**, including **cash paid for Property, Plant, and Equipment
(PP&E)**.

However:

-   **"Capital Expenditures" is not a formally defined GAAP metric**
-   It is an **analyst-derived metric based on GAAP financial
    statements**

  Item                               GAAP?
  ---------------------------------- ----------------------------------
  Cash paid for PP&E                 Yes
  "CapEx" metric used by investors   No (derived from GAAP reporting)

So CapEx is **based on GAAP data but not itself a GAAP-defined metric.**

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 3. How CapEx Is Calculated

If the cash flow statement does **not explicitly list CapEx**, analysts
estimate it using the **PP&E roll-forward from the balance sheet.**

### Formula

CapEx = Ending PP&E − Beginning PP&E + Depreciation

### Why this works

Depreciation **reduces PP&E over time**, while CapEx **increases PP&E**.

By adding depreciation back, we can isolate the value of **new assets
purchased during the period.**

### Example

  Item             Amount
  ---------------- ----------
  Beginning PP&E   \$900M
  Ending PP&E      \$1,000M
  Depreciation     \$150M

CapEx = 1000 − 900 + 150 = **\$250M**

This means the company purchased **\$250M of new long-term assets**.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 4. Relationship to Free Cash Flow

CapEx is critical because it determines **Free Cash Flow (FCF)**.

### Formula

FCF = Operating Cash Flow − CapEx

Example:

  Metric                Amount
  --------------------- --------
  Operating Cash Flow   \$500M
  CapEx                 \$200M

FCF = 500 − 200 = **\$300M**

This represents the cash a company generates **after reinvesting in its
business.**

Free cash flow is widely used in:

-   valuation models (DCF)
-   equity research
-   private equity analysis
-   value investing

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 5. Important Nuances

CapEx is **usually close to "purchase of PP&E", but not always
identical.**

Potential differences can come from:

-   Asset sales (selling equipment)
-   Acquisitions that include fixed assets
-   Capitalized software development costs
-   Capitalized R&D
-   Finance leases

Because of this, analysts often review:

-   PP&E footnotes
-   investing cash flow breakdown
-   capitalized development cost disclosures

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 6. Warren Buffett's Interpretation of CapEx

Warren Buffett and Charlie Munger emphasize a key distinction:

## Maintenance CapEx vs Growth CapEx

Not all capital spending is equal.

### Maintenance CapEx

Money required to **keep the business operating at its current level.**

Examples:

-   Replacing worn-out machines
-   Updating factory equipment
-   Maintaining infrastructure

This spending is **necessary just to maintain current earnings.**

### Growth CapEx

Spending used to **expand the business and increase future earnings.**

Examples:

-   Building a new factory
-   Expanding production capacity
-   Opening new locations

This spending is **optional and intended to grow revenue.**

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 7. Why This Distinction Matters

Most financial statements **do not separate maintenance vs growth
CapEx.**

But investors care deeply about this difference.

Example:

  Company     CapEx    Maintenance CapEx   Growth CapEx
  ----------- -------- ------------------- --------------
  Company A   \$200M   \$180M              \$20M
  Company B   \$200M   \$60M               \$140M

Both report the same CapEx, but:

-   **Company A** must reinvest heavily just to stand still
-   **Company B** generates excess cash and is investing for growth

This is why Buffett focuses on **Owner Earnings**.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 8. Buffett's Owner Earnings Formula

Buffett approximates true cash generation using:

Owner Earnings =\
Net Income\
+ Depreciation & Amortization\
− Maintenance CapEx\
− Working Capital Needs

Because **maintenance CapEx is rarely disclosed**, analysts often
approximate it as:

Maintenance CapEx ≈ Depreciation

This assumption works reasonably well for **stable businesses**, but not
for rapidly growing companies.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 9. Key Takeaways

-   CapEx represents **cash spent on long-term assets**
-   It appears in the **investing section of the cash flow statement**
-   It is **derived from GAAP reporting but not itself a GAAP metric**
-   It is often similar to **purchases of PP&E**
-   It plays a critical role in calculating **Free Cash Flow**
-   Sophisticated investors separate **maintenance CapEx vs growth
    CapEx**
-   This distinction drives how investors estimate **true business cash
    generation**
